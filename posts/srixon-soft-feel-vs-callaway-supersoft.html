Srixon Soft Feel vs Callaway Supersoft: What the Makers Publish, and What the Robot Found

Two two-piece ionomer balls at about two dollars each, both sold on softness. One maker prints its compression number and one does not, and the independent gauges tell a more interesting story than either.

The short answer

They are closer than the marketing suggests. Both are two-piece ionomer balls, both sit in the lowest-spin and lowest-flight group in MyGolfSpy's robot tests, and both are among the slowest off the driver at mid swing speeds. Srixon publishes its compression, 58, on the spec sheet; Callaway publishes nothing, and the gauges that have measured the Supersoft read 41, 44 and 47 depending on who measured it and which year. Buy the Soft Feel if you want the spec you were promised and two dollars a dozen back. Buy the Supersoft if your driver speed is slow: at 85 mph it finished third-longest of 44 balls in the 2025 robot test, and it was second-fastest off a 7-iron in 2026. Neither is a spin ball, whatever the box says.

Both of these balls are bought by people who want soft feel without paying urethane money, and both makers know it. Callaway's page says "exceptional soft feel, control, and spin from tee-to-green." Srixon's says "soft without compromise." Both sentences are doing a lot of work, so here is what each company actually prints, followed by what the independent testers actually measured.

The SheetWhat each maker publishes

The table has one empty cell that matters. Srixon puts a compression number on its sheet. Callaway does not, on the product page, the yellow-ball page or the family page. The "38" that circulates for the Supersoft is from a Today's Golfer review of the 2017 ball, and it has been copied forward through four generations since.

The MeasurementWhat the gauges say the Supersoft actually is

So the Supersoft is softer than the Soft Feel by every gauge that has touched it, and less consistent than its own marketing. A box that ranges from 40 to 54 in compression is a box with two different balls in it. Srixon's number for the Soft Feel has moved too, from 60 across the tenth to thirteenth generations to 58 on the fourteenth, but at least it is Srixon's own number, and Srixon prints it on the Amazon listing as well as its site.

The RobotWhat the tests found

MyGolfSpy runs the only large robot test that includes both balls, at Golf Laboratories in Scottsdale, 44 balls in 2025. Its findings put them in the same corner of the field.

Read the two 2026 rows together and you have the whole argument. Off the driver at mid speed, both balls are slow, and the Supersoft is the slowest of all. Off a 7-iron, the Supersoft is second-fastest. That is not a contradiction; it is what a soft, low-spin ionomer ball does. It gives up speed on the fastest strike and gives some back on the slower, more lofted one, which is why the low-speed driver result in 2025 came out third-longest. If you swing fast, the sheet is telling you to buy something else. If you swing slowly, it is telling you the Supersoft's claim about speed is not empty, just narrow.

The DisagreementClaim against measurement

None of this makes either ball bad. Golf Digest's 2026 Hot List gives both a silver, at "$25", and describes the Supersoft's feel as "pillowy" and the Soft Feel as "soft feel without distance loss." Golf Monthly called the Supersoft "unbelievable value" and the Soft Feel "an ideal ball for those who like a good quality softer ball at a competitive price." What the tests take away is the idea that either one spins. They are low-spin, low-flight balls for golfers who want the ball to go straight and feel soft, and both do that.

The PickWhich one, for whom

Soft Feel if you want to know what you are buying. Srixon prints the number, prints it on the retail listing, and the ball costs two dollars a dozen less at the makers' own prices. The 14th generation also comes in a Lady version at 56 and in three Brite colours.

Supersoft if your driver speed is on the slow side and you want the extra yards the low-speed robot found, or if you want the softest thing on the shelf and can live with the box-to-box variation Ball Lab keeps finding. It is also the one with the most independent data behind it, which is a strange kind of recommendation, but a real one: you know more about its flaws than about the Soft Feel's.

What neither is: a ball for a fast swinger or for anyone who chose it for spin. If that is you, the compression chart is where the makers' published numbers for the whole market sit side by side, and soft versus distance balls covers the trade you are actually making.

FAQQuick answers

Which is softer, the Srixon Soft Feel or the Callaway Supersoft?

The Supersoft, by every independent gauge. Srixon publishes 58 for the Soft Feel. Callaway publishes no number, and the readings taken on the Supersoft are 41 (Today's Golfer, 2025), 44 (MyGolfSpy, 2026) and 47 (MyGolfSpy, 2023 model).

What compression is the Callaway Supersoft?

Callaway does not publish one. The 38 quoted everywhere comes from a 2017 review of an older generation. Independent readings on the 2023 and 2025 balls are 47, 41 and 44, with MyGolfSpy finding balls in one box ranging from about 40 to 54.

Which goes further?

It depends on your speed. In MyGolfSpy's 2025 robot test the Supersoft was third-longest of 44 balls at low driver speed and second-shortest at high speed. In 2026 both balls were among the four slowest off the driver at mid speed, and the Supersoft was second-fastest off a 7-iron. Srixon's Soft Feel was not singled out for distance in either year.

Do these balls spin around the green?

Less than the boxes suggest. Both were among the lowest-spinning balls with the driver in 2025, and the Supersoft's 35-yard wedge spin was 4,381 rpm, which MyGolfSpy described as low. No wedge figure is published for the Soft Feel. They are two-piece ionomer balls; that is the trade.

Is the Srixon Soft Feel 58 or 60 compression?

Srixon listed 60 for the tenth through thirteenth generations and lists 58 for the fourteenth, the 2025 ball, with the Lady version at 56. No independent gauge reading has been published for either.

Which is cheaper?

The Soft Feel, by two dollars a dozen at the makers' own prices: $24.99 against $26.99 in September 2026. Golf Digest lists both at $25 on its 2026 Hot List, and street prices move.
