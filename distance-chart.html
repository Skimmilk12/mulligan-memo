Golf Club Distance Chart: How Far Should You Hit Each Club?

The honest answer to golf's most-Googled question — plus why these numbers are rough averages, not promises, and how to find the only distances that actually matter: yours.

The short, honest answer

Four organisations actually measure how far golfers hit it and publish the results: Shot Scope, Arccos, TrackMan and The R&A/USGA. Their numbers do not support the chart that circulates online. Every measured driver average lands under 226 yards — the R&A's laser-measured club golfers at 215.6, Arccos men at 225.0 — against the 230 to 250 usually published, and 76% of amateurs average under 250 off the tee. Through the bag, no measured source publishes a full set of clubs, and the two biggest datasets disagree with each other by 19 yards on a scratch golfer's 7-iron. What follows is what each of them actually publishes, labelled with who was measured and whether the figure is carry or total.

"How far should I hit each club?" is one of the most searched questions in golf, and it deserves a straight answer rather than a tidy little table pretending to be science. The truth is that distance varies enormously from golfer to golfer. Two players with the same handicap can hit a 7-iron 30 yards apart, and the same player can hit it 20 yards apart on two different swings. So this chart does something most don't: it tells you the honest range to expect, names the things that move it, and then spends as much time showing you how to find your own numbers as it does handing you averages.

Please read this — it's the whole point

The numbers below are rough averages, not guarantees. They are not measured from your swing, and they should never be treated as a precise prescription or a target you've failed to hit. Real distance depends on:

  • Swing speed — the single biggest factor, and the reason ranges are so wide.
  • Strike quality — a center hit can carry 15–20% farther than a thin or toe strike.
  • Conditions — altitude, temperature, wind, and turf firmness all shift carry and roll.
  • Equipment & loft — modern strong-lofted irons fly farther than the same "number" did 20 years ago, so a "7-iron" isn't a fixed thing.

Use this as a starting reference and a gapping sanity check — not as the final word on your game.

Ground RulesRead this before the chart

Two definitions make the chart actually useful. First, carry vs. total. The numbers below are carry distance — how far the ball flies before it lands — not total distance with roll. Carry is what you can control and what you should club for; roll is a gift from firm turf and downslopes that you can't count on. A driver might roll out 10–25 yards on a dry fairway, while a high wedge rolls almost nothing.

Second, "average amateur" is doing a lot of work. Published averages skew toward men who play enough to log data, so we've split the chart into three honest columns — a shorter hitter, an average amateur, and a longer hitter — and given separate ranges for women and seniors below. Find the column that sounds like you, and even then, expect to land somewhere inside the range rather than dead on a number.

The ChartWhat the measured data actually shows

Four organisations measure golfer distance at scale and publish the results themselves: Shot Scope and Arccos track amateurs on the course through GPS and club sensors, TrackMan measures tour professionals by radar, and The R&A and USGA publish laser and ShotLink data. Everything below comes from one of those four. Where they disagree — and they do — both figures are shown.

Off the tee: every measured average is below the chart you have seen

The circulating chart tends to put the average golfer at 230 to 250 yards off the tee. Every measured population mean lands under it.

Shot Scope also publishes the spread behind its average, and the spread is the more useful number: 19% of amateurs average under 200 yards, 76% average under 250, and only 2% average over 300.

The R&A's laser measurements make the same point more starkly. Measuring UK club golfers off the same tees in the same competitions in 2018, they found the 10th percentile at 159 yards, the median at 219, and the 90th percentile at 266. Anyone comparing themselves against one number in a table is comparing themselves against a fiction.

Shot Scope publishes driver, 7-iron and a single 3-wood figure. Arccos publishes more, but as medians of what golfers actually hit rather than averages of every shot. Both are below.

Basis not stated on the page; Arccos states at platform level that its distances are total, “based on where your ball ‘ends up’.” Smart Distance “automatically excludes mishits and abnormally long shots,” so these describe a well-struck shot rather than a raw average.

The two biggest amateur datasets disagree. On a scratch golfer's 7-iron, Shot Scope says 178 and Arccos says 159 — nineteen yards apart, from the two companies with the most tracked shots in golf. Neither is wrong on its own terms; they measure and filter differently. But they cannot both go in one row, which is exactly what every aggregated chart does to them.

One thing worth saying plainly, because it cuts against the easy story: the generic iron numbers are not badly wrong. The familiar “7-iron, 140 to 150 yards” is roughly where measured 10-to-20 handicap golfers actually sit. What is wrong is the pretence of a single row — measured 7-iron distance runs from 178 down to 132 across the handicap ladder, a 46-yard spread that one line hides.

Two caveats on everything above. Loft is not standardised, so a “7-iron” from a modern game-improvement set is stronger-lofted than a classic 7-iron of the same name and will carry meaningfully farther — see our guide to loft for why the number on the sole is not the whole story. And these datasets are not a random sample of golfers. Arccos and Shot Scope users are paying subscribers who chose to track their game, and neither company addresses that self-selection anywhere. The R&A's amateurs were UK club golfers at six venues, measured up to 2019.

The Real FindingThe club you pull vs the shot you hit

This is the most useful thing in any of the measured data, and almost nobody publishes it. Arccos records two numbers for every approach shot: the yardage the golfer was facing when they pulled the club, and how far the ball actually went. The gap between those two widens sharply as handicap rises.

Arccos, published 2020. Read this carefully: the gap is not pure optimism. It also contains mishits, deliberate lay-ups, and shots played to a spot rather than at the flag. But the direction and the slope are unambiguous, and they come from the club-selection records of a company measuring hundreds of millions of shots.

A 20-handicapper reaching for a 5-iron from 187 yards is, on the evidence, about 35 yards short of the green.

That is a more valuable insight than any row in a distance chart. The common error is not misjudging the yardage to the pin — rangefinders solved that. It is choosing the club you hit once, on your best strike, rather than the one you hit on a normal Tuesday.

There is a related finding from The R&A worth putting beside it: in 2019, golfers in the 21-and-above handicap category used a driver on 96.7% of measured tee shots, while scratch-band golfers used one on 84.5%. The players who benefit least from the longest club in the bag reach for it most.

The Other NumberCarry vs. total distance: how much roll to add

Every number in the chart above is carry — and the first question people ask is "so what's my total distance?" The honest answer: it depends on the club and the turf, but the pattern is predictable. Low-lofted clubs come in shallow and hot, so they roll a lot; high-lofted clubs drop steeply with spin, so they stop nearly where they land. Here's the roll you can typically add to carry on a normal, dry fairway — halve it on soft ground, and add more on baked-out summer turf.

Club groupTypical roll to addExample (average amateur)
Driver15–30 yards210 carry → ~225–240 total
Fairway woods10–20 yards195 carry → ~205–215 total
Hybrids & long irons8–15 yards170 carry → ~180–185 total
Mid irons (5–7)5–10 yards140 carry → ~145–150 total
Short irons (8–9)3–6 yards120 carry → ~123–126 total
Wedges0–5 yards100 carry → ~100–105 total

This is why lasering your ball at rest overstates what you "hit" that club: you measured carry plus a roll that changes course to course. Club for carry, enjoy the roll when it comes.

The Real DriverDistance by swing speed

If the wide ranges above frustrate you, here's the variable doing most of the work: club head speed. In the common amateur band — roughly 85–105 mph — a well-struck driver carries about 2.3–2.6 yards per mph of club head speed. The relationship isn't perfectly linear, though: launch efficiency tends to fall off at slower speeds, and the best strikers squeeze out more than the ratio at high speeds. Here are honest speed-specific carry ranges from published launch-monitor reference data:

Driver swing speedTypical driver carryRoughly matches
70 mph125–155 yardsMany beginners, slower senior & women's swings
80 mph160–190 yardsAround a 25 handicap on the Shot Scope ladder
90 mph195–225 yardsAround a 15–20 handicap
100 mph225–255 yardsAround a 5 handicap or better
110+ mph255–290+ yardsLow-single-digit & tour-level speed

Two useful corollaries. First, if you know your driver speed from a range session or fitting, you can sanity-check your whole bag — iron speeds fall off roughly 2–3 mph per club as shafts get shorter, which is exactly why carry steps down in those tidy 10–15-yard gaps. Second, chasing distance through speed is real but slow; finding distance through better strike and fit is faster, which is the whole argument of our slow-swing-speed ball guide and shaft flex explainer.

One thing the chart quietly shows: even when the mid-points step down cleanly, the ranges overlap heavily from club to club — the top of one club's range routinely reaches into the next club up. That's the whole point of showing ranges instead of single numbers: averages collide at the edges, and your real gaps are the only ones worth trusting. That brings us to the two things you should actually do with this page.

Do ThisHow to find YOUR real distances

This is the most important section on the page. A chart of averages is a conversation starter; your own measured carries are what you actually club with. There are three reliable ways to get them, from most precise to most realistic.

Free printable yardage card →

Blank on purpose — fill in your own carries, print two bag-sized copies per sheet, or save it as a PDF.

  • Launch monitor (most precise). A range session or fitting on a launch monitor gives you exact carry numbers for every club in under an hour, and separates carry from roll so you're not fooled by a hot bounce. It's the gold standard. You don't need to own one — many ranges and golf shops have them — but if you want to practice with data at home, see our best launch monitors under $500.
  • Rangefinder or GPS (most realistic). Measuring real shots on the course captures how you actually swing under pressure, with real lies and nerves — which is usually a bit shorter than your range "showroom" numbers. One honest caveat: lasering the spot where your ball came to rest gives you total distance (carry plus roll), not carry — and a GPS app shares the caveat, since it measures to wherever you're standing, which is the ball at rest. To measure carry, shoot the pitch mark or the spot where the ball first landed — easiest on soft fairways and greens that hold a mark — or let a launch monitor separate carry from roll for you. Track both over several rounds: carry is what clears the bunker; total is where you'll putt from. Our guides to the best rangefinders under $100 and rangefinder vs. GPS cover the tools.
  • Shot tracking over a season (most thorough). Sensor or app-based tracking logs every shot you hit and averages them automatically, smoothing out the good and bad strikes into a number you can trust. It takes time but needs no effort once it's set up.
The mistake almost everyone makes

Do not club off your best-ever strike. Everyone remembers the one 7-iron they flushed 165 — and then comes up short on that club for the rest of their life. Your usable distance is your typical, repeatable carry: the shot you'd bet on landing eight times out of ten, not your highlight reel. Measure several shots per club, throw out the outliers, and use the middle.

The Real LessonWhy gaps matter more than raw yards

Here's the part the average-distance question usually misses entirely. How far you hit each club matters far less than how evenly your clubs are spaced. The whole point of carrying 14 clubs is to have a club for every yardage you'll face. If two clubs go the same distance, one of them is wasted; if there's a 25-yard hole between two clubs, every shot that lands in that hole becomes an awkward half-swing guess.

Good gapping usually means roughly 10–15 yards between full-swing clubs — tighter through the scoring clubs, a bit wider through the long clubs. Most amateurs, when they finally measure, discover two problems: their long irons bunch together (a 4-iron and 5-iron that carry within a few yards of each other), and there's a yawning gap between their pitching wedge and sand wedge that a gap wedge was invented to fill.

The fixes are cheap relative to the strokes they save. A hybrid in place of a hard-to-hit long iron restores a real distance step at the top of the bag. A properly chosen wedge setup closes the holes at the bottom — and our roundup of the best wedges under $100 shows you don't need to spend tour money to do it. If you're still assembling a bag, our take on how many clubs a beginner actually needs and the best beginner sets under $500 both lean on this same gapping logic.

So chase distance if it's fun — and the right distance ball or well-fit irons can genuinely help — but understand that clean, consistent gaps will lower your scores more reliably than five extra yards off the tee ever will. FAQQuick answers

What is the average driver distance for an amateur golfer?

For the average male amateur, driver total distance commonly lands somewhere around 215–230 yards, with carry typically in the 200–220 yard range. Slower swingers often carry 170–200 yards, and stronger amateurs carry 240+ yards. Women amateurs average roughly 140–175 yards of carry. These are broad averages — your own number depends heavily on swing speed, strike quality, and how much roll the course gives you, so treat any single figure as a ballpark, not a target.

How far does the average golfer hit a 7-iron?

There is no single answer, and the measured data is the reason. Shot Scope’s tracked-shot averages run from 178 yards at scratch down to 132 at a 25 handicap — a 46-yard spread. Arccos, measuring the median distance a 7-iron actually travelled, publishes 159 for a scratch golfer, 148 at 10 and 140 at 20. The two disagree at the scratch end by 19 yards. If you want one band, 140 to 150 is roughly where measured 10-to-20 handicap golfers sit. The 7-iron is the club most people use as their personal yardstick, but there's huge spread between players, so the only number that matters for your game is the one you actually carry on the course.

Are these golf club distances carry or total distance?

The main chart on this page lists carry distance — how far the ball flies through the air before it lands — because carry is what you actually control and what you should club for. Total distance adds roll, which varies enormously with turf firmness, slope, and wind. A driver might add 10–25 yards of roll on a dry fairway, while a high-flying wedge rolls almost nothing. When you measure your own distances, record carry.

Why are the distances on this chart shown as ranges instead of exact numbers?

Because exact, guaranteed per-club numbers don't exist. How far you hit each club depends on swing speed, angle of attack, strike quality, ball, loft, shaft, altitude, temperature, wind, and turf. Any chart that gives you a single precise number for everyone is selling false precision. Ranges are honest: they tell you the neighborhood to expect, and then you narrow it down by measuring your own carry distances.

Should I worry more about how far I hit each club or the gaps between them?

The gaps. Raw distance is fun to talk about, but consistent gaps — usually 10–15 yards between full-swing clubs — are what let you cover every yardage on the course without two clubs that go the same distance or a big hole between clubs. Most amateurs have at least one overlap (often two long irons or wedges that carry nearly the same) and at least one gap. Fixing gapping with a gap wedge, a hybrid, or a loft adjustment improves scoring more than chasing five extra yards off the tee.

How far should I hit my driver for my swing speed?

In the common amateur band of roughly 85–105 mph, a well-struck driver carries about 2.3–2.6 yards per mph of club head speed — roughly 160–190 yards of carry at 80 mph, 195–225 at 90, and 225–255 at 100. The relationship isn't perfectly linear: efficiency falls off at slower speeds, and elite strikers beat the ratio. Strike quality decides where you fall in any band — a centered strike at 90 mph routinely outflies a mishit at 100.

Does loft affect how far each golf club goes?

Loft is the main reason each club goes a different distance: less loft means a flatter, longer flight. It's also why charts disagree with each other — modern "strong-lofted" irons put a 7-iron label on what's close to an older 6-iron's loft, so the same swing carries it farther. When comparing yourself to any chart, the loft on the sole matters more than the number stamped on it.

How do I find out how far I actually hit each club?

Three good options. A launch monitor at a range or fitting gives you precise carry numbers for each club in one session — the gold standard. A rangefinder or GPS lets you measure real on-course shots over time, which captures how you actually play under pressure — just remember that shooting the ball at rest gives you total distance; laser the spot where it first landed to get carry. And shot-tracking systems or apps log every shot you hit over a season and average them. For real, usable numbers, ignore your single best strike and use a typical, repeatable carry.
